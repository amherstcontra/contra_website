The latest Contra post, "On Being a College" (hereafter, OBC), will not prevent current AI users from stopping their use. It does not barricade any future users from starting it.

OBC takes the rather uncontroversial opinion of "AI is bad" and tries to make it as charged as possible. While the article begins by setting the scene of a misguided approach to AI in academia, its final third alludes to a much broader scope of users. The guilty connotations of “cheating” in school are amplified and redirected to instigate a witch hunt against general users of AI.

A culture of shaming slams the door on honest, productive discourse that can actually convince people not to use AI. The sententious rhetoric and message deepens the wedge between users and non-users, intensifying the polarization present among peers.

AI is scary in its uncertainty and consequences, and the corporations behind it are ominous. And it deserves space for us "liberal arts" students to have serious conversations about it without feeling like we're participating in something taboo.

We're getting a lot of mixed messages on AI right now. On the one hand, many professors have no-AI policies in their syllabi and have switched to blue-book exams. On the other hand, the college gave us Gemini and other AI tools with no real guidance and a blanket statement on the "important questions for higher education." Also, Google search is now AI, meaning we have to go the extra mile just to have the option not to interact with it.

The “right” path of AI use has yet to exist. While we must reckon with this conundrum, an article that calls for this witch hunt helps nobody. At best, this will lead to ambivalence; at worst, it will polarize shamers against users, a net-negative.

A culture of shame reads as ingroup fighting. The AI we are familiar with now is a corporate product, made for and promoted to us to be hooked. The call to action in OBC concentrates blame and consequences on students–the consumers–and doesn’t offer adequate space to address the systemic problems that influence our usage.

For those trying to understand and adapt to AI, here are some things to consider:

- To bypass the automatic AI overview, type -ai after your search, or -[any letter that's not in your query].
- Ask your professor how they would operate with this technology if they were a student in current times.
- Get involved in IRL discussions and action. For example, a group of Amherst students went to Boston on October 3rd to rally for the Mass Wins Act, which advocates for transparency and third-party evaluations on AI models.
